# What venture investors select for, and how this skill adapts it

Early-stage investors such as Y Combinator, Sequoia and a16z have screened tens of thousands of startups, and their criteria are the best public record of what separates ideas that grow from ideas that stall. This skill borrows their criteria but not their goal. A venture fund needs a few outsized outcomes and staffs up to get them; this builder needs a place where fast, cheap, unattended prototypes can find real demand and keep compounding. Read this file before scoring (Stage 5), red-teaming (Stage 7) or judging (Stage 8), so you know why each criterion exists and can handle edge cases.

Snapshot of sources, October 2026. These are stable principles, not time-sensitive facts; current funding rounds and accelerator themes still come from `landscape.md`.

## 1. What investors look for

| Principle | What it means | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Founders first | Determination, flexibility, speed and founder-market fit weigh more than the idea, because ideas change and founders don't. "Is this a good idea for my team?" | [F: Paul Graham, "What We Look for in Founders", 2010] [F: J. Friedman, YC Startup School, "How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas", 2022] |
| Organic ideas beat brainstormed ones | At least 70% of YC's top 100 companies found their idea through the founders' own work or experience. Deliberate brainstorming tends to land on the same tarpit ideas everyone else has. | [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| A well, not a puddle | A small group that urgently needs something beats a large group that mildly wants it. Ask "who wants this right now, even in a rough form?" | [F: Paul Graham, "How to Get Startup Ideas", 2012] |
| Acute problem | Strong problems lack good solutions and force painful workarounds. Solutions in search of a problem (starting from the technology) are the most common mistake. | [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Why now | Something recently changed (technology, regulation, behaviour) that makes the idea possible or necessary. "Live in the future, then build what's missing." | [F: Graham, 2012] [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Tarpit avoidance | Some ideas look easy and obvious but have stayed unsolved for a structural reason. Find who tried, and name the barrier, before committing. | [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Competition is a signal | Competitors prove the problem is real. What matters is a sharp insight they lack. | [F: Graham, 2012] [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Proxy | A company succeeding with a similar model in another market, segment or region is evidence the model works. | [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Schlep and boring spaces | Tedious, unglamorous problems have higher hit rates because others avoid them. | [F: Graham, 2012] [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Software, not headcount | Revenue that grows with headcount is a services business. | [F: Friedman, 2022] |
| Sell the work | AI products that sell a completed outcome get better and cheaper with every model release; tools risk being absorbed by the model. Start with work buyers already outsource (an existing budget line), and with tasks that are mostly rule-based and verifiable ("intelligence") rather than taste ("judgement"). | [F: Sequoia, J. Bek, "Services: The New Software", 2026-03] |
| Workload-model fit | The first product to solve a stubborn, high-value job with a newly capable model can lock in an unusually loyal first cohort. The window is short: it lasts until the next model makes the job easy for everyone. | [F: a16z, "The Cinderella Glass Slipper Effect", 2025-12] |
| Retention over hype | Early AI cohorts are inflated by "AI tourists". Investors rebase retention to month 3 and read later retention against that committed base. Durable growth with solid retention beats spikes with fragile retention. | [F: a16z, "Retention Is All You Need", 2025-09] [F: Bessemer, "State of AI", 2025-08] |
| Defensibility | Thin wrappers over public APIs are discounted. Investors reward data that improves with use, deep workflow embedding and distribution others can't copy. | [I: from a16z, Sequoia and Bessemer above] |
| Where capital is going | YC publishes Requests for Startups each batch. The Fall 2026 list includes AI-native compliance, small-software hosting, multiplayer AI, self-maintaining APIs, consumer AI agents and AI for ageing populations. | [F: ycombinator.com/rfs, Fall 2026] |

Their process is a funnel: a high-volume written screen read quickly by partners, a short interview probing clarity, evidence of demand and founder-market fit, then a decision. They expect most bets to fail and look for the few outliers. This skill's stages mirror that funnel, but the final selection differs (section 3).

## 2. Where this builder deliberately differs

| Investors | This builder | Consequence for the skill |
|---|---|---|
| Need a market large enough for a fund-returning outcome | Need a market large enough to be worth years of focus, not a billion-dollar one | #8 checks willingness to pay and the size of the prize, but never estimates TAM. Areas too small for venture money are a feature: less funded competition. |
| Fund teams that hire to do "things that don't scale" | Operates alone with a hard weekly operating-load cap | #4 stays a knockout. "Sell the work" only counts where the output is verifiable without a human checking each one. |
| Pick by conviction, because they can't run experiments | Can run cheap experiments in days | The judge recommends an opening round across finalists and lets the probe results pick the focus area. |
| Want near-term growth metrics (weekly growth, ARR) | Patient; no near-term revenue needed | Patience is an edge: areas whose payoff compounds slowly (search traffic, community trust, accumulated data) score higher on #10, because impatient competitors avoid them. |
| Follow the Requests for Startups and the hot categories | Should treat hot categories with care | Heavy funding in a category validates the job but crowds the head-on product shapes. #6 rewards the edges funded teams leave: too small, too regional, too fragmented or too early. |

| Rubric criterion | Investor principle behind it |
|---|---|
| #1 Acute, recurring pain | A well, not a puddle; acute problem; who wants this right now |
| #2 AI step change and model tailwind | Solution to a real problem, not a technology in search of one; sell the work, so model releases help rather than absorb you |
| #3 Signal speed | Make something people want, measured by behaviour; fast iteration |
| #4 Low-ops fit | Software, not headcount (tightened for a solo operator) |
| #5 Pivot room and compounding | Flexibility; defensibility from assets that accumulate |
| #6 Room for a small builder | Competition as signal; tarpit avoidance; where capital is crowding |
| #7 Why now and trajectory | Why now; live in the future; workload-model fit window |
| #8 Upside | Market size and existing spend, adapted to a patient builder |
| #9 Founder fit | Founder-market fit; organic ideas |
| #10 Advantage fit | Unfair advantage, defined by this builder's speed, low-ops model and patience |

## 4. Questions a partner would ask of any area

Use these as a final check before writing a score or a verdict. If you can't answer one with evidence, say so; that gap is usually what the first probe should test.

1. Who wants this right now, and what are they doing about it today?
2. What changed recently that makes this possible, and how long before it is easy for everyone?
3. Who has tried this before, and why did they stall?
4. What does the builder know or have that a funded team of ten doesn't?
5. When the next model ships, does the product get better or get absorbed?
6. What accumulates with each probe that makes the next one cheaper or stronger?
7. What would have to be true for this to be worth two years of focus?
